Shipping a product you built feels like crossing a finish line. Then you realize the finish line was actually the starting gun. Here's what the first real launch taught me.

## Users break everything in surprising ways

I tested my app thoroughly. Within a day of real users, I'd seen failures I never imagined: an emoji in a name, a huge pasted blob, a click on a button I thought was disabled. Lesson: your test data is clean; reality isn't. Plan for the unexpected.

## "Done" is a moving target

As soon as something ships, you see ten ways to improve it. That's not a failure — it's information. The trick is to stop the perfectionism and ship the next improvement instead of endlessly polishing the current one.

## Bugs are feedback, not failure

Every bug report is a gift. It's someone telling you exactly what to fix, for free. The worst product is one where nobody cares enough to report anything.

## The boring stuff matters more

Real users don't care about your clever architecture. They care about:

- **Speed** — is it slow?
- **Clarity** — do I know what to do?
- **Reliability** — does it break?

None of that is glamorous. All of it is what determines whether people come back.

## The lesson that stuck

The product I was proudest of engineering wasn't the one users loved. The one they loved was the one I'd kept simple and made reliable. That reframed everything: my job isn't to impress with complexity. It's to make the simple thing work flawlessly.

If you're about to ship, here's the honest advice: launch earlier than feels comfortable. Real feedback beats your assumptions every single time.
